on cheating

2026-05-18

Cheating is treated as an unusually taboo topic across the internet. In many communities, anything perceived as even remotely “cheating-adjacent” is met with immediate hostility, regardless of the context or technical value behind it.

This phenomenon has always interested me. Nearly everyone proclaims their disdain for cheaters, yet millions upon millions of people engage with various forms of unfair advantages every day.

A possible explanation for this paradox is a kind of selective indifference toward one's own behavior. Many of those who vehemently express their hatred for people who seek unfair advantages are often not so “clean” themselves. This distinction is rarely examined because society is full of small, normalized advantages that sit in a grey area.

In reality, people routinely engage in these advantages: exploiting systemic loopholes, selectively interpreting rules and guidelines, leveraging unequal information, or relying on resources that are technically allowed but not realistically accessible to their competition. Success, regardless of context, is often defined by how effectively someone can navigate these asymmetries.

I like to examine politicians to demonstrate this, as they tend to represent the extreme of this advantage-seeking behavior. The same kind of behavior that is so easily condemned in abstract discussion (or moral thought experiments) often reappears in political systems, just scaled up and justified through “necessity.”

People often assume a clear divide between “good actors” and “bad actors” in politics, but in reality most decisions are made under pressure: incentives, loyalty, self-preservation, and affiliation all compete with perceived moral obligation (the source of which is often irrelevant in practice).

A useful example of this is a variation of the trolley problem where a person must choose between saving a family member or a larger group of strangers. In theoretical discussions, many claim that they would choose the utilitarian option and save the greater number of lives. But in practice, where the stakes are real and immediate, most people will intuitively prioritize the person with whom they have a close personal connection.

The point of this examination is not to condemn the action, but to reveal the gap between how people describe their decision-making framework and what actually drives their decisions under pressure. Humans are not impartial, and our biological nature will often override whatever self-conception we may hold of ourselves.

Returning to the political metaphor, what people label as corruption or hypocrisy in politicians can be interpreted less as an exception to human nature and more as its logical extension. People often fail to recognize that, when placed under similar conditions, many of the decisions they are quick to criticize would likely be the same ones they themselves would make.

People are reluctant to imagine themselves making the same decisions they view as indefensible, even when those decisions arise from pressures they consistently operate under.

Once this gap between self-perception and actual behavior is acknowledged, it becomes easier to understand why cheating in games is such a revealing case study: it makes incentive structures explicit and removes much of the moral ambiguity found in real-world systems.

Cheating in games has therefore become a socially safe target. It is easy to label cheating as unacceptable because the rules are explicit, but much harder to apply the same scrutiny to one’s own behavior in contexts where advantage-seeking is normalized.

This societal contempt risks flattening a technically rich domain into a purely moral one. “Cheating” becomes a label that ends discussion rather than a subject that invites analysis, making it difficult to examine what is actually happening beneath the surface.

From a programming perspective, this divide is obvious. What is socially categorized as cheating is, in practice, an interesting and technically demanding problem space. When you move beyond the social framing, you uncover a set of engineering problems involving system behavior, constraints, and unintended interactions.

My experience has made it difficult to accept the default framing of cheating as something purely illegitimate or “wrong,” and I think that as a programmer, you should be careful about outsourcing your judgment on these topics entirely to social consensus.

I also believe that building cheats, or even engaging in basic reverse engineering of games, can make you a better software engineer and a more capable systems thinker. This claim is based less on the act itself and more on the technical constraints it forces you to operate under.

Everything you have ever built as a programmer is typically constructed from the inside out: systems designed with full knowledge of their structure and intended behavior. In contrast, reverse engineering forces you to work from the outside in—you must reconstruct internal behavior without access to its assumptions or design intent.

This inversion develops a specific kind of systems thinking that is rarely exercised in traditional development. Rather than learning a narrow set of techniques, you develop a general intuition for how real systems behave in practice. You repeatedly confront the gap between how systems are described and how they actually function.

Reverse engineering also removes documentation and intended usage patterns by definition. You are left with only observable behavior and low-level structure, and must build a working mental model from incomplete information. Over time, this strengthens your ability to infer structure from behavior, reason about unknown systems, and identify patterns in how complexity emerges.

Ultimately, this is not just about games or cheating. It is about learning how to understand systems that you did not design, cannot fully see, and were never meant to interact with directly. That skill transfers far beyond any single domain, and becomes increasingly valuable as the systems you work with grow in complexity and opacity.
